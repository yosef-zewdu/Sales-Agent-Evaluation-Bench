# Synthesis Memo — Preference Leakage

**Paper:** Li et al., "Preference Leakage: A Contamination Problem in LLM-as-a-Judge" (2025, arXiv:2502.01534)  
**Author:** Yosef Zewdu  
**Date:** 2026-04-29

---

## Summary

Li et al. formally define **preference leakage**: a contamination problem that arises when the LLM used for training-data generation (LLMG) and the LLM used for evaluation (LLMJ) are related — either the same model, in an inheritance relationship, or within the same model family (Section 3.2, Equations 3–6). The paper's core finding is that this relatedness causes judges to systematically favor outputs from their "student" models, not because those outputs are better, but due to shared stylistic and formatting fingerprints. The empirical results (Table 1) report Preference Leakage Scores (PLS) of 28.7% on Arena-Hard and 23.6% / 21.9% on AlpacaEval 2.0 for cross-family related pairs. The paper also finds that preference leakage is more severe for subjective questions than objective ones, and that judges show near-random accuracy on recognition tasks involving related model outputs — making the bias "subtler and harder to detect" than egocentric bias (Section 4.3). The paper closes by calling for future work on detecting, preventing, and mitigating preference leakage, without proposing a specific technical remedy.

---

## Design Choice I Disagree With

**The paper's framing: preference leakage is studied as a threat to *evaluation of trained models*, not as a threat to *dataset authoring*.**

Li et al.'s threat model (Section 3.2) posits a student model trained on LLM-generated data, then evaluated by a related judge. The contamination pathway they study is: data generator → student model → judge evaluates student model output. This is the right framing for leaderboard evaluation (someone submits a model fine-tuned on GPT-4 data, and GPT-4 judges it), but it misses an equally acute risk that arises in *generative benchmarks* where the "chosen" side of preference pairs is itself produced by a frontier LLM during dataset construction.

In Tenacious-Bench v0.1, the "chosen" outputs in preference pairs come from two sources: (1) successful Week 10 execution traces (fixed, no authoring risk), and (2) rewrites generated by a frontier model to correct constraint violations. Source (2) creates a direct generator→judge leakage risk at the *authoring stage*: if the rewriting model and the judge backbone are from the same family, the trained judge will score those rewrites higher not because they are more signal-grounded or tone-compliant, but because they share the generator's stylistic fingerprint. The paper's PLS framework measures this contamination only *after* the student model is trained — it cannot detect authoring-stage leakage because there is no "student model" yet, only a preference pair under construction.

This is a strictly harder version of the problem. The paper's recommendation — use cross-family evaluation — covers only the final judgment node. It does not address leakage at the rewrite-production node, the variation-expansion node, or the seed-authoring node. Each of these nodes in the Tenacious-Bench authoring DAG can introduce leakage if not explicitly rotated.

Concretely: the paper reports PLS of 23.6% for GPT-4o (generator) and Gemini-1.5 (judge) pairs on Arena-Hard (Table 1). This number represents post-training evaluation leakage. For Tenacious-Bench, if Claude Sonnet 4.6 generates the "chosen" rewrites and then Claude is used as the dev-tier quality filter, the stylistic fingerprint is embedded in the training data — meaning the trained Qwen judge will be optimized against Claude-flavored "chosen" examples and will generalize poorly to non-Claude outputs. The paper's threat model would classify this as a Type 2 inheritance relationship (Equation 5), but their experimental setup doesn't test this specific authoring-stage pipeline.

**Where my design goes further:** the rotation table in `methodology.md` enforces model-family separation at every authoring stage — seed generation, variation expansion, rewrite production, and quality filtering — not just at final evaluation. This is a response to the paper's empirical finding that PLS persists across model families even without direct inheritance (same-family PLS: 23.6%), which implies that proximity in stylistic space is sufficient to create leakage even without a shared training-data lineage.

## How I Applied (and Departed From) This Paper

Applied:
- The core PLS finding is taken as ground truth: same-family generator/judge pairs introduce systematic bias. The rotation table in `methodology.md` directly prevents this at every stage.
- The paper's appendix discussion of stylistic and format-level leakage (not just content) informs why Claude Sonnet 4.6 is excluded from the dev-tier judge role even though it is the eval-tier model: Claude generates much of the "chosen" rewrite data, so using Claude as the bulk judge on the same data would reproduce the exact leakage scenario the paper quantifies.

Departed:
- The paper does not address **authoring-stage leakage** in generative benchmarks. I extend their threat model to cover it, which required defining a rotation policy across all authoring DAG nodes.
- The paper calls for future work on mitigation without proposing a specific mechanism. For Tenacious-Bench, the mitigation is operationalized: hard-fail gates in `scoring_evaluator.py` eliminate the worst constraint-violating outputs before the LLM judge scores them, which reduces the surface area where stylistic leakage can inflate scores on substantively bad outputs.
